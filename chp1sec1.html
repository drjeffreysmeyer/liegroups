    Section 1.1 Topological Groups

    In this section we introduce the theory of topological groups which will later aid us in our study of Lie groups. The prerequesites for this chapter are basic point-set topology and basic group theory, which can be found in Munkres and Dummet and Foote respectively.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.1.1 Point-Set Topology of Topological Groups

    Definition 1.1.1.

    A topological group \(G\) is a group with a Hausdorff topology such that the maps \(\mu\colon G\times G\to G\) and \(\iota:G\to G\) given by \(\mu(x,y) = xy\) and \(\iota(x) = x^{-1}\) are continuous. Here \(G\times G\) is given the product topology.
    🔗
    🔗
    For each \(g\in G\) left (resp. right) multiplication by \(g\) is also a homeomorphism of \(G\) whose inverse is given by left (resp. right) multiplication by \(g^{-1}\text{.}\) As inversion is an involution (i.e. \(\iota^2=Id\)), it is also a homeomorphism.
    🔗

    Example 1.1.2.

    The “topological fields” \(\R\text{,}\) \(\C\text{,}\) and the \(p\)-adics \(\Q_p\) provide a wealth of examples. Let \(k\) be one of these fields. Then \(k\) with addition and \(k^\times\) with multiplication are topological groups. The vector space \(k^n\) with the product topology is a topological group. In particular, \(M_n(k)\cong k^{n^2}\) is a topological group. Furthermore multiplication in \(M_n(k)\) is also continuous. (Check this!) Then \(GL_n(k)\text{,}\) which is an open subset, forms a topological group with respect to multiplication.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.3.

    Many important topological groups are matrix groups, such as \(GL_n(\R)\text{,}\) \(O_n\text{,}\) \(SO_n\text{,}\) \(GL_n(\C)\text{,}\) \(U_n\text{,}\) and \(SU_n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.4.

    Any group given the discrete topology is a topological group.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.1.5 Nonexample - The Zariski Topology.

    \(\R\) with the Zariski topology is not a topological group as it is not Hausdoff. Show \(\Z\) is Zariski-dense in \(\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.1.6.

    A subset \(E\subseteq G\) is said to be symmetric if whenever \(x\in E\text{,}\) we have \(x^{-1}\in E\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Let \(G\) be a topological group, and \(U\) a neighborhood of the identity \(e\text{.}\) Then \(U^{-1}\) is also a neighborhood of the identity, and thus \(U\cap U^{-1}\) is a symmetric neighborhood of \(e\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Lemma 1.1.7.

    Any neighborhood of an \(x\in G\) is of the form \(xU\) for some neighborhood \(U\) of \(e\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proposition 1.1.8.

    Let \(U\) be a neighborhood of \(e\text{,}\) and \(n\) a positive integer. Then there is a neighborhood \(V\) of \(e\) such that \(V^n = \underbrace{V\cdots V}_{n \text{ times}}\subseteq U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    The multiplication map \(\varphi\colon G\times\cdots\times G\to G\) is continuous. Therefore \(\varphi^{-1}(U)\) is a neighborhood of \((e,\ldots,e)\text{.}\) We may then choose neighborhoods \(V_1,\ldots,V_n\) of \(e\) such that \(V_1\times\cdots\times V_n\subseteq \varphi^{-1}(U)\text{.}\) Set \(V = V_1\cap\cdots\cap V_n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.1.9.

    Let \(X\) a topological space, and \(Y\subseteq X\text{.}\) We say that \(Y\) is locally closed in \(X\) if for all \(y\in Y\) there is a neighborhood \(N_y\) of \(y\) in \(X\) such that \(N_y\cap Y\) is closed in \(N_y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proposition 1.1.10.

    Let \(X\) be a topological space with \(Y\subseteq X\) a subspace. Then \(Y\) is locally closed if and only if \(Y = O\cap C\) for some open set \(O\subseteq X\) and some closed set \(C\subseteq X\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    (\(\Longrightarrow\)): For each \(y\in Y\text{,}\) let \(N_y\) be an open neighborhood of \(y\) such that \(N_y\cap Y\) is closed in \(N_y\text{.}\) Then \(U_y = N_y\setminus Y\) is open in \(N_y\) for each \(y\text{,}\) and hence open in \(X\text{.}\) In particular, \(U = \bigcup_y U_y\) is open in \(X\text{,}\) and \(U\) is contained in \(O = \bigcup_y N_y\text{.}\) But \(Y = O\setminus U\text{,}\) so \(Y\) is closed in \(O\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (\(\Longleftarrow\)): Clear.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.11.

    Closed and open subsets are locally closed.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.12.

    A locally compact subset of a Hausdorff space is locally closed. Let \(X\) be Hausdorff and \(Y\subseteq X\) be locally compact. For \(y\in Y\) there exists a neighborhood \(N_y\subseteq X\) of \(y\) such that \(\ol{N_y}\cap Y\) is compact in \(Y\text{.}\) Then \(\ol{N_y}\cap Y\) is closed in \(X\) and hence \((\ol{N_y}\cap Y)\cap N_y=N_y\cap Y\) is closed in \(N_y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Lemma 1.1.13.

    Let \(G\) be a topological group and \(H\subseteq G\) a subgroup. Then \(\ol{H}\subseteq G\) is a subgroup.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Pick \(x,y\in \ol{H}\) and let \(U\) be a neighborhood of \(xy^{-1}\text{.}\) It suffices to show \(\ol{H}\cap U\) is nonempty. By the continuity of multiplication, there exist neighborhoods \(V\) of \(x\) and \(W\) of \(y^{-1}\) such that \(VW\subseteq U\text{.}\) Since \(x,y\in\ol{H}\text{,}\) \(V\cap H\) and \(W^{-1}\cap H\) are nonempty. Pick \(g\in V\cap H\) and \(h\in W^{-1}\cap H\text{.}\) Then \(gh^{-1}\in H\cap VW \subseteq H\cap U\text{.}\) Therefore \(xy^{-1}\in\ol{H}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Lemma 1.1.14.

    Let \(G\) be a topological group with \(H\subseteq G\) a subgroup. If \(H\) is open, then \(H\) is closed.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Since \(H\) is open, each translate \(Hg\) is open for \(g\in G\text{.}\) Using coset representatives \(g\text{,}\) we have that \(G = \bigsqcup_g Hg\text{,}\) so that in particular \(H = G\setminus \bigsqcup_{g\neq 1} Hg\text{.}\) As each \(Hg\) is open, \(H\) is closed.
    🔗
    🔗

    Corollary 1.1.15.

    If \(G\) is a connected topological group, then any open subgroup \(H\subseteq G\) must be \(G\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Corollary 1.1.16.

    A locally closed subgroup of a topological group is closed.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(H\leq G\) be locally closed. By replacing \(G\) with \(\ol{H}\text{,}\) we may without loss of generality assume that \(H\) is dense in \(G\text{.}\) By Proposition Proposition 1.1.10, there exists an open set \(O\subseteq G\) and a closed set \(C\subseteq G\) such that \(H = O\cap C\text{.}\) As \(H\) is dense in \(G\) and \(C\) contains \(H\text{,}\) we can conclude that \(C = G\text{,}\) so that \(H = O\text{.}\) The result then follows from Lemma Lemma 1.1.14.
    🔗
    🔗

    Lemma 1.1.17.

    Let \(G\) be a topological group and let \(N\subseteq G\) be a subset with nonempty interior. Then \(H = \langle N\rangle=\{\text{Subgroup Generated by } N\}\) is an open subgroup of \(G\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(O\) be a nonempty open subset of \(N\text{,}\) and let \(x\in O\text{.}\) Then for all \(h\in H\text{,}\) \(hx^{-1}O\) is an open neighborhood of \(h\) contained in \(H\text{.}\) Thus \(H\) is open.
    🔗
    🔗

    Corollary 1.1.18.

    A connected topological group \(G\) is generated by any subset with a nonempty interior. In particular, any neighborhood of \(e\) generates \(G\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.1.19.

    Let \(G\) be a topological group and \(H\subseteq G\) a subgroup. Let \(G/H\) be the space of left cosets and let \(\pi\colon G\to G/H\) be the projection map. The quotient topology on \(G/H\) is the finest topology such that \(\pi\) is continuous. In particular, a subset \(E\subseteq G/H\) is open if and only if \(\pi^{-1}(E)\subseteq E\) is open.
    🔗
    🔗

    Lemma 1.1.20.

    Let \(G\) be a topological group, and \(H\leq G\text{.}\) Then \(\pi\colon G\to G/H\) is an open map.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose that \(U\subseteq G\) is open. Then so is \(hU\) for all \(h\in H\text{,}\) so that \(\bigcup_{h\in H} hU = HU\) is open in \(G\text{.}\) But \(\pi^{-1}(\pi(U)) = UH\text{,}\) so it must be that \(\pi(U)\) is open.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proposition 1.1.21.

    Let \(G\) be a topological group and let \(H\subseteq G\) be a subgroup. Then \(G/H\) is Hausdorff if and only if \(H\) is closed.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    (\(\Longrightarrow\)): Since \(G/H\) is Hausdorff, one point sets are closed. In particular, \(\{e\cdot H\} = \{H\}\) is closed, so that \(\pi^{-1}(\{H\}) = H\) is closed in \(G\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (\(\Longleftarrow\)): Let \(f\colon G\times G\to G\) be the map \((x,y)\mapsto x^{-1}y\text{.}\) Then \(f\) is continuous, so \(f^{-1}(H)\) is closed in \(G\times G\text{.}\) But \(f^{-1}(H) = \{(x,y)\mid xH = yH\}\text{.}\) Thus if \(xH\neq yH\) in \(G/H\text{,}\) \((x,y)\notin f^{-1}(H)\text{,}\) and thus there exist open neighborhoods \(U\) and \(V\) of \(x\) and \(y\) respectively such that \((U\times V)\cap f^{-1}(H) = \emptyset\text{.}\) By Proposition Proposition 1.1.21, \(\pi(U)\) and \(\pi(V)\) are open neighborhoods of \(xH\) and \(yH\text{,}\) respectively. Furthermore, it is easily seen that the condition \((U\times V)\cap f^{-1}(H) = \emptyset\) forces \(\pi(U)\cap \pi(V) = \emptyset\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Exercises

    In each of the following exercises, let \(G\) be a topological group unless stated otherwise.
    🔗

    1. Seperation Conditions.

    In our definition of a topological group, we assumed \(G\) to be Hausdorff. In this exercise you will show we could have assumed something much weaker than Hausdorff but the group structure will give us something much stronger than Hausdorff.
    🔗
    1. Let \(G\) be group with a topology in which points are closed and multiplication and inversion are continuous. Show that \(G\) is a topological group.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose that \(H \subset G\) is a closed subgroup. Show that \(G/H\) is regular. In particular \(G\) is regular.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. (\(\star\)) Show that \(G\) is completely regular. (Hint: Adapt the proof of the Urysohn lemma.)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Let \(H\subset G\) be a subgroup. Show that \(H\) is open if and only if \(G/H\) is discrete.
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Suppose \(A\) and \(B\) are subspace of \(G\) such that \(A\) is closed and \(B\) is compact. Show that \(AB\) closed.
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Suppose that \(H\subset G\) is a compact subgroup. Show that the quotient map is closed.
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Suppose that \(H\subset G\) is a compact subgroup and that \(G/H\) compact. Show that \(G\) is compact.
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    Show that the connected component of \(G\) containing the identity, denoted \(G^0\text{,}\) is a normal subgroup.
    🔗
    🔗

    7.

    Suppose that \(G\) is first countable and is either separable or Lindelof. Show that \(G\) is second countable.
    🔗
    🔗

    8.

    Suppose that \(G\) is locally compact and \(H\subset G\) is a subgroup. Show that \(G/H\) is locally compact.
    🔗
    🔗

    9.

    Suppose that \(G\) is locally compact and connected. Show that \(G\) is paracompact.
    🔗
    🔗

    10.

    Show that \(\pi_1G\) is an abelian group.
